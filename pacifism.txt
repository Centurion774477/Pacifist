
Pacifism — coined by the French peace campaigner 
Émile Arnaud at the tenth Universal Peace Congress in Glasgow, 1901 —
can be loosely defined as the opposition to war or violence.

Though Pacifism covers a wide spectrum of views and has a variety of sects,
some core beliefs are believing international disputes should be resolved
peacefully instead of violently; rejecting the use of violence and force to obtain
political, economic, or social goals; and an unconditional rejection of any and all forms of warfare.

There are also some more radical definitions of Pacifism — 
anarchist or libertarian pacifism (also referred to as Anarcho-Pacifism), for example,
believes not only in the opposition of state authority, but
the rejection of that organization without any force or violence, 
compared to other anarchist schools, which may do so with force.

Anarcho-Pacifists also reject the principle of violence, 
claiming it is a form of power and is therefore contradictory to key anarchist
ideals like the rejection of dominance or hierarchy.

Religion and Pacifism go like bread and butter, and have been a key vector to the spread of the philosophy.

Christian history is rich with other examples of pacifism, 
like the Popes denouncing conflicts and Martyrs choosing death instead of violence, 
and even the Bible, with quotes like Matthew 26:52:
'Then Jesus said to him, "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword."'

Though Christianity is the most notable example,
many other religions also practice pacifism, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism.

Pacifism has had an extremely rich history,
from the Warring States Period in China to Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr,
Pacifism seems to be a pretty resilient philosophy that
— despite not the most popular philosophy — is here to stay for centuries to come.
